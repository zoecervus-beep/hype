## 1. "7 neighbours, 6 republics, 5 nations, 4 languages, 3 religions, 2 alphabets, 1 Tito"

**Verdict:** ✅ This is a real and widely repeated folk saying, not an official formula. Many versions circulate.
- The order and the items vary. Last items seen include "1 party" (`1 PARTIJA`), "1 Tito", "1 boss" and "1 Yugoslavia". A version attributed to Tito reverses the counts ("two alphabets, three languages, four religions…"). None of these versions can be traced to a documented Tito quote.
- A reputable-press example is the Baltimore Sun, 26 Jun 1991: "two alphabets, three main religions, four official languages, five major nationalities and six constituent republics."
- Standard Serbo-Croatian forms, with correct numeral agreement: `7 suseda` / `7 susjeda` (alternative `7 granica`, "7 borders") · `6 republika` · `5 naroda` · `4 jezika` · `3 vere` / `3 vjere` · `2 pisma` · `1 Tito` / `1 partija`. All are grammatical as written.
- What each number means:
  - **7 neighbours:** Italy, Austria, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Greece, Albania. Correct.
  - **6 republics:** Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, Macedonia. Serbia also had 2 autonomous provinces, Vojvodina and Kosovo.
  - **5 nations:** Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, Macedonians, Montenegrins. Caveat: Muslims (today's Bosniaks) were recognised as a sixth nation from 1968, and the 1971 census was the first to count them as one. "5" is therefore the 1945–68 picture. That is fine for a saying.
  - **4 languages:** usually read as Serbian, Croatian, Slovene and Macedonian. Some versions have Serbo-Croatian, Slovene, Macedonian and Albanian. Officially, Serbo-Croatian/Croato-Serbian counted as one language, so the federal South Slavic languages were three. Albanian, Hungarian and others were official regionally.
  - **3 religions:** Orthodox, Catholic, Muslim.
  - **2 alphabets:** Latin and Cyrillic (JUGOSLAVIJA / ЈУГОСЛАВИЈА).

**Use on screen:** the countdown exactly as in the SPEC. Caption for 4: "4 languages". Caption for 3: "3 faiths".

**Sources:**
- Baltimore Sun, 26 Jun 1991: https://www.baltimoresun.com/news/bs-xpm-1991-06-26-1991177040-story.html
- Muslims recognised as a nation, 1968/1971 (Indiana UP, *Nationalism and Federalism in Yugoslavia 1962–1991*, ch. 9): https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/nationalism-and-federalism-in-yugoslavia-1962-1991/section/120b9dc9-8aaa-4af4-a31b-cac893f71b6a
- Britannica, Bosnia in communist Yugoslavia: https://www.britannica.com/place/Bosnia-and-Herzegovina/Bosnia-and-Herzegovina-in-communist-Yugoslavia

## 2. Tito's funeral, 8 May 1980

**Verdict:** ⚠️ Adjust. Tito died 4 May 1980 in Ljubljana. The funeral was in Belgrade on 8 May 1980.
- **Countries represented:** the figures conflict.
  - **128** of the 154 UN members, as given by Wikipedia and Balkan Insight (2010: "209 delegations from 128 countries… some 700,000 people").
  - **127** in several regional sources ("209 delegacija iz 127 zemalja").
  - **123** in TIME's contemporaneous report (May 1980: "Official mourners came from 123 countries").
  - A US embassy telegram of the time (FRUS) speaks of "almost 100 high-level foreign delegations".
  - **Recommendation:** use **"120+"**, which every source supports. Neither 127 nor 128 is established.
- **Breakdown**, as usually cited (Wikipedia): 4 kings, 6 princes (some sources say 5), 31 presidents, 22 prime ministers, 47 foreign ministers, plus delegates from 7 multilateral organisations, 6 movements and 40 political parties. TIME in 1980 gave 4 kings, 32 presidents and other heads of state, and 22 prime ministers. Safe wording: "4 kings, 30+ presidents, 22 PMs".
- **"One of the largest state funerals in history":** defensible (National Library of Scotland: "regarded as one of the largest state funerals to ever take place"). Do **not** say "the largest ever". John Paul II's funeral in 2005 drew more heads of state and government.

**Use on screen:** `1980. · 120+ ZEMALJA`, caption "Tito's funeral: 120+ countries". Alternative: `4 KRALJA · 30+ PREDSEDNIKA` (`PREDSJEDNIKA`), caption "4 kings, 30+ presidents".

## 4. Tito–Stalin split, 1948, and the "stop sending people to kill me" note

**Verdict:** ✅ for the split. ❌ The note is not authenticated, so do not show it as fact.
- **The split:** the Cominform resolution expelling the CPY is dated 28 June 1948 (Vidovdan). The CPY Central Committee rejected it on 29 June 1948. This is the "historic NO" (`istorijsko NE`).
- **The note:** it is known only second-hand. Khrushchev reportedly told Aleksei Snegov about it, and Snegov told Roy Medvedev, who published it in R. and Zh. Medvedev, *The Unknown Stalin* (2003). Their version dates it to about 1950, not "June 1948". No archival original has been published, and the wording differs between retellings. Status: unverified and possibly apocryphal.
- **What is documented:** Soviet plans to assassinate Tito. The MGB/Beria "agent Max" (Iosif Grigulevich) plan of 1952–53, found by Dmitri Volkogonov in the archives, is published by the Wilson Center.

**Use on screen:** `1948. — NE.`, caption "Yugoslavia says no to Stalin". If you want the assassination angle, use `STALJIN JE PLANIRAO ATENTAT` (Stalin planned his murder, 1952–53), which is documented. Do not show the note.

- Britannica, Cominform: https://britannica.com/topic/Cominform

## 5. Workers' self-management law, 1950

**Verdict:** ✅ Correct.
- **Date:** adopted by the People's Assembly of the FNRJ on **27 June 1950**. Tito presented it in a speech on 26 June, which is why some English sources give 26 June.
- **Official name:** *Osnovni zakon o upravljanju državnim privrednim preduzećima i višim privrednim udruženjima od strane radnih kolektiva* ("Basic Law on the Management of State Economic Enterprises and Higher Economic Associations by Work Collectives"). Published in Službeni list FNRJ no. 43/1950. Popularly known as the "self-management law".
- **Slogan of the time:** "Fabrike radnicima!" (Croatian: "Tvornice radnicima!").
- **Context:** the first experimental workers' council was at the Solin cement works in late 1949. Between August and October 1950, 7,136 workers' councils were elected.

**Use on screen:** `FABRIKE RADNICIMA`, caption "1950: factories to the workers".

**Sources:**

## 6. Youth work actions

**Verdict:** ✅ Correct, with the corrections to the numbers below.
- **Brčko–Banovići, 1946:** 92 km, built 1 May to 7 November 1946 and finished ahead of schedule. **~62,000** Yugoslav youth took part (62,268 is the figure usually cited), plus about 1,800–2,200 foreign volunteers. Known as the first federal youth work action.
- **Šamac–Sarajevo, 1947:** **242 km**, built 1 April to 15 November 1947 and opened 16 November 1947 with Tito present. **~211,000** youth (211,381) in 887 brigades, including foreign brigades; one of those was led by E. P. Thompson. Round it to "200,000+".
- **Autoput "Bratstvo i jedinstvo":** the Zagreb–Belgrade section is **382 km**, built 1948–1950 and opened 27 July 1950. It was built mainly by youth brigades, with the army (JNA) taking part. Volunteer counts range from about 200,000 (academic article, *Histories of Postwar Architecture*) to about 300,000 (wider estimates, some covering later phases). **Leave the number out, or use "200,000+".**

**Use on screen:**
- `BRČKO–BANOVIĆI · 92 KM`, caption "1946: 62,000 young volunteers".
- `ŠAMAC–SARAJEVO · 242 KM`, caption "1947: 200,000+ youth".
- `AUTOPUT BRATSTVO I JEDINSTVO`, caption "Zagreb–Belgrade, 382 km, 1948–50".

## 7. Economic growth

**Verdict:** ⚠️ Adjust. Use "~6% a year", with the period stated.
- **By decade:** average annual GDP (social product) growth was **9.5% in 1953–59, 6.6% in the 1960s and 6.1% in the 1970s** (Yarashevich & Karneyeva, *Communist and Post-Communist Studies* 46(2), 2013).
- **Kukić** (*European Review of Economic History*, 2018; LSE working paper 268): Yugoslavia was "the fastest growing socialist economy in the post-WWII era and … one of the fastest growing countries in Europe during the 1950s and the 1960s". GDP per working-age person grew 3.6% a year over 1952–1989, against 2% in the US.
- **Do not** attribute "6.1% 1960–80" to "World Bank 1991". That line circulates without a traceable primary citation.

**Use on screen:** `RAST ~6% GODIŠNJE`, caption "~6% growth a year, 1953–79". For something punchier: `1953–79: RAST 6%+`.

**Sources:**
- Yarashevich & Karneyeva 2013: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0967067X13000111
- Kukić, LSE working paper: https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/WorkingPapers/Economic-History/2017/WP268.pdf

## 8. Literacy and life expectancy

**Verdict:** ✅ Correct.
- **Illiteracy (population aged 10+):**
  - 1948 census: **25.4%**. It ranged from 2.4% in Slovenia to 44.8% in Bosnia and Herzegovina (Bondžić 2010, *Andragoške studije*).
  - 1981 census: **9.5%** (*Istorija 20. veka*, 2023).
  - For comparison, 1921 (Kingdom): 51.5%.
- **Life expectancy at birth, 1948 → 1981:**
  - Men: 48.6 → 67.7 years.
  - Women: 53.0 → 73.2 years.
  - Around 1948, both sexes together: about 51 (50.9).
  - These are Yugoslav statistical data as reproduced in the sources. Kunitz (AJPH 2004) documents improving and converging mortality up to the 1980s.
  - Rounded: **~50 → ~70**.

**Use on screen:** `NEPISMENOST 25% → 9,5%`, caption "Illiteracy, 1948 → 1981". `ŽIVOTNI VEK ~50 → ~70` (`VIJEK`), caption "Life expectancy, 1948 → 1981".

## 9. The Yugoslav ("red") passport

**Verdict:** ⚠️ Adjust. Do not say "visa-free everywhere".
- From the mid-1960s Yugoslavia signed dozens ("scores") of visa-free agreements. They eventually covered **almost all European states in both blocs** and over a hundred Non-Aligned states.
- Notable exceptions included the **USA**, Albania, China and Israel.
- Anthropologist Stef Jansen: during the late Cold War it "allowed wider and smoother cross-border mobility than any other passport known".
- **Best phrasing:** "Yugoslavs could travel without visas to most of Europe, East and West, and much of the world."

**Use on screen:** `CRVENI PASOŠ`, caption "Visa-free: most of East & West".

**Sources:**
- Jansen 2009, *JRAI*: https://rai.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9655.2009.01586.x
- Jansen 2009, PDF: https://personalpages.manchester.ac.uk/staff/stef.jansen/documents/sj09-redpassport.pdf

## 15. Zastava 750 "Fićo" and Yugo 45

**Verdict:** ✅ Correct.
- **Fićo:** built in Kragujevac under Fiat 600 licence from **18 Oct 1955 to 18 Nov 1985**. **923,487** were made (early cars as the Zastava 600; the 750 name dates from 1962). The Serbian nickname is "Fića"; Croatian and Bosnian use "Fićo".
- **Yugo 45:** series production started **28 November 1980**. Volume production ramped up in 1981.

**Use on screen:** `FIĆO · 923.487`, caption "Zastava 750, 1955–85". `YUGO 45 · 1980.`, caption "Yugo enters production".

## 16. Liberation

**Verdict:** ✅ Correct.
- **Belgrade:** liberated **20 October 1944** by the Partisans and the Soviet Red Army.
- **End of the war:** the final operations ended with the Battle of Poljana (Carinthia/Slovenia) on **15 May 1945**, the last battle of WWII in Europe.
- **Army size:** the Partisan army, renamed the Yugoslav Army on 1 March 1945, numbered **~800,000** by April–May 1945, organised in four armies. It had been about 300,000 at the end of 1943 (Britannica) and about 650,000 in late 1944.
- **Avoid** "largest resistance movement in Europe". That is contested, for example against the Polish Home Army.

**Use on screen:** `BEOGRAD SLOBODAN · 20. 10. 1944.`, caption "Belgrade liberated". `800.000 BORACA`, caption "~800,000 Partisans by 1945".

## 17. Slogans and the oath

**Verdict:** ✅ Correct.
- **"Smrt fašizmu – sloboda narodu"** ("Death to fascism, freedom to the people"): the Partisan slogan and salute from 1941. It appears in the first issue of the Supreme HQ *Bilten* on 16 August 1941. Its exact first use is undocumented. It became iconic through Stjepan Filipović's shout at his execution in Valjevo in 1942. In use, one person says "Smrt fašizmu!" and the reply is "Sloboda narodu!".
- **"Bratstvo i jedinstvo"** ("Brotherhood and unity"): a wartime Partisan/CPY slogan that became the official principle of inter-ethnic policy in socialist Yugoslavia.
- **"Druže Tito, mi ti se kunemo (da sa tvoga puta ne skrenemo)"** ("Comrade Tito, we swear to you that we will not stray from your path"):
  - The oath verse is wartime folk poetry. A 1982 press account traces it to Kozara in 1942 (Persa Ristić, village of Bjelajci). Treat that attribution as traditional.
  - The famous song is **Zdravko Čolić's 1980** Jugoton single: music by Đorđe Novković, lyrics by Mira Alečković. It was released in April 1980, just before Tito's death.

**Use on screen:** `SMRT FAŠIZMU – SLOBODA NARODU`, caption "Death to fascism – freedom to the people". `BRATSTVO I JEDINSTVO`, caption "Brotherhood and unity". `DRUŽE TITO, MI TI SE KUNEMO`, caption "Comrade Tito, we swear to you".

## 18. Official state names

**Verdict:** ✅ Correct, with detail added.
- **FNRJ** (Federativna Narodna Republika Jugoslavija): proclaimed **29 Nov 1945** by the Constituent Assembly. Its constitution was adopted **31 Jan 1946**.
- **SFRJ:** from the constitution of **7 Apr 1963**.
- **End:** on **27 Apr 1992**, the SFRJ Assembly's Federal Council adopted the constitution of the FR Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro). The Badinter Commission's Opinion No. 8 of **4 Jul 1992** held that the SFRY's dissolution was complete.
- **"SFRJ 1945–1992"** is common shorthand for socialist Yugoslavia. Strictly, the name "SFRJ" covers 1963–1992. Alternatives: `JUGOSLAVIJA 1945–1992`, or `FNRJ · SFRJ 1945–1992`.

**Use on screen:** `SFRJ 1945–1992` is acceptable as the hype-edit outro. For a pedantic-proof version use `FNRJ 1945 · SFRJ 1963 · 1992`.
